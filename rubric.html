The open rubric

Read exactly what we measure.

JAI-Assay scores every row on three independent dimensions, each on a 1–5 scale, against the published rubric below. Nothing here is a black box: the same rubric the judges read is the rubric you read.

version R2.2 scale 1–5 integer dimensions 3 temperature 0.0

The dimensions

Score honestly — length, verbosity, and hedging earn nothing. A short, evidence-bound row scores the same as a long one with identical grounding.

01

Faithfulness

requires source documents

Does the row stick to facts present in the cited evidence?

  • 5Every claim traces directly to evidence; no fabrication.
  • 4Claims are inferred-but-plausible from evidence; no contradictions.
  • 3Minor unsupported detail, or one inference that’s a stretch.
  • 2Meaningful unsupported claims, or a clear contradiction with evidence.
  • 1Fabricated identifiers, invented facts, or wholly ungrounded.
02

Specificity

Does the row capture concrete, non-obvious facts?

  • 5Pulls a specific, training-useful fact — numbers, names, edge cases, mechanisms.
  • 4Solid specifics, but could be one step more concrete.
  • 3Generic but accurate restatement of the source.
  • 2Vague paraphrase; the source had more to offer.
  • 1Hedge-filled or content-free.
03

Utility

Would training on this row teach a model a transferable pattern?

  • 5High-leverage example — the kind of row you’d want lots of.
  • 4Useful pattern, slightly narrow.
  • 3OK but unremarkable; training value is replaceable.
  • 2Pattern is trivial or already well-represented.
  • 1Anti-pattern — would teach bad behavior.

Scoring discipline

calibration

Match the anchor, apply its score

Each row is scored by matching it to the calibration anchor whose pattern it most resembles, then applying that anchor’s expected score. The full 1–5 scale is used as the anchors define it — scores are never pushed toward any target distribution. A genuinely strong dataset may concentrate at 4–5; a genuinely weak one at 1–2. The anchors are ground truth.

reasoning

Reasoning first, score second

Reasoning is written in full before any number is assigned — the judge names the lowest-scoring dimension and the specific weakness that drove it, then states the integer. No pre-committing to a score and rationalizing after.

Calibration examples

Worked examples that define what the numbers mean. (Illustrative — shown with neutral domains.)

Q: What was the duplicate charge amount, and when was it refunded?
A: The customer was charged $49 twice on 2026-05-01; the second charge was refunded within 3–5 business days, preserving the billing cycle.
faithfulness 5 specificity 5 utility 5

Every claim traces to the cited evidence; the answer is specific (exact amount, date, window) and teaches a reusable pattern. A high-leverage row.

Q: How should the service handle a failed webhook delivery?
A: It retries with exponential backoff up to 5 attempts, then moves the event to a dead-letter queue surfaced under delivery_failed.
faithfulness 5 specificity 5 utility 4

Faithful and specific — names the actual mechanism and state, not “an error.” Utility is 4, not 5, because the pattern is narrow: you wouldn’t want dozens of near-identical rows about this one path.

Q: What’s a good way to handle errors in async jobs?
A: Always log errors and surface them to the user with appropriate context so they can understand what went wrong and take corrective action.
faithfulness 4 specificity 2 utility 2

Generic-but-true, so faithfulness is 4. But specificity is 2 — hedge-filled, no concrete mechanism (no status code, no field, no example). Utility is 2: training on this teaches a model to give platitudes when asked a specific question.

How a verdict is reached

no sources, no faithfulness

We say what we couldn’t measure

Faithfulness can only be scored against source documents. Submit without them and we score specificity and utility, mark faithfulness as skipped, and label the result partial — never silently.

agreement, not authority

Certified means the judges agreed

A green certified mark requires a 3-judge panel, every dimension scored, no schema failures, and the judges landing within a point of each other on at least 90% of rows. The agreement statistic is reported on every run — and recomputable from the per-row scores.
